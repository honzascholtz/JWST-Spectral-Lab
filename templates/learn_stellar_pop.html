A galaxy's spectrum is a fossil record

A galaxy's light is the combined glow of billions of individual stars of every age, plus glowing gas and, often, a haze of dust. Crucially, a star's colour and brightness change predictably over its life: young, massive stars burn hot and blue; older, lower-mass stars glow cooler and redder. That means the overall shape of a galaxy's spectrum — how much light it puts out at each wavelength — is a kind of fossil record of every generation of stars that ever formed inside it, still shining together today. Astronomers use this fact to work backward from a measured spectrum to the physical story of a galaxy: how much mass is locked up in stars, how those stars have been forming over cosmic time, and how much interstellar dust is dimming and reddening the light along the way.

Building a model galaxy

This lab uses Bagpipes, a stellar population synthesis code used in real JADES research, to build a model spectrum from a small number of physical ingredients:

The ingredients of a model spectrum

  • Stellar mass — the total mass ever turned into stars, which mostly sets the overall brightness.
  • Age & star formation history — when the galaxy formed most of its stars. This lab uses a "delayed" history: star formation rises, peaks, then declines, controlled by an age and a decline timescale (τ).
  • Metallicity — the fraction of elements heavier than hydrogen and helium in the stars, which subtly changes their colours.
  • Ionisation parameter (log U) — how strongly the galaxy's young, hot stars are ionising surrounding gas, which controls how bright its emission lines glow.
  • Dust attenuation (Av) — how much interstellar dust dims and reddens the light, using the Calzetti dust law measured from real star-forming galaxies.

Change any of these and the whole model spectrum reshapes — dust preferentially removes blue light, making a galaxy look redder and fainter; adding older stars makes the spectrum redder without touching the emission lines; more mass raises the whole curve. Because several of these effects push the spectrum in similar directions, disentangling them is a genuine scientific challenge — a red galaxy might be old, or dusty, or both, and only the detailed shape of the spectrum can tell you which.

How the lab works

1
Choose a real JWST galaxy spectrum. Each button loads real NIRSpec data for a galaxy from the JADES survey, already placed at its known, spectroscopically-confirmed redshift.
2
Adjust the six sliders — mass, ionisation, metallicity, age, decline timescale, and dust — and watch the red model spectrum redraw over the black observed data in real time.
3
Watch the Star Formation History panel. As you move the age and τ sliders, this panel shows how your model galaxy's star formation rate has risen and fallen since the Big Bang — a direct visualisation of your model's implied history.
4
Check the Star-Forming Main Sequence. Real star-forming galaxies fall along a tight relationship between stellar mass and star formation rate. This panel plots your model galaxy against that relation, telling you whether it behaves like a typical star-forming galaxy, a quiescent (retired) galaxy, or a starburst.
5
Minimise the score. The title bar reports a goodness-of-fit score comparing your model to the data, accounting for the measurement noise — the lower it is, the more closely your physical story matches what JWST actually saw.

Why it matters

This kind of spectral fitting is exactly how astronomers turn a JWST spectrum into the physical properties reported in scientific papers: galaxy masses, ages, dust content, and star formation rates across cosmic time. Comparing these properties across thousands of galaxies at different redshifts is how we reconstruct the overall history of star formation in the universe — when it peaked, how it has declined, and how dust has shaped what we're able to see.

Star formation history (SFH)
How a galaxy's rate of forming new stars has changed over cosmic time.
Metallicity
The abundance of elements heavier than helium, relative to the Sun.
Dust attenuation
The dimming and reddening of starlight caused by interstellar dust grains.
Star-forming main sequence
The tight observed relationship between a galaxy's stellar mass and its star formation rate.
