The universe is expanding — and stretching light with it

Almost a century ago, Edwin Hubble noticed that distant galaxies are all moving away from us, and the further away a galaxy is, the faster it appears to recede. We now understand this as a consequence of the expansion of space itself: as light from a distant galaxy travels toward us across billions of years, the space it's travelling through is stretching, and the light's wavelength stretches along with it. Light that left a galaxy as blue or ultraviolet light can arrive at JWST stretched all the way into the infrared. Astronomers call this stretching redshift, denoted z, because longer wavelengths shift toward the red end of the spectrum.

Redshift as a distance — and time — machine

A redshift of z means a wavelength has been stretched by a factor of (1 + z). Because we know how fast the universe expands, a measured redshift tells us both how far away a galaxy is and how long ago we're seeing it — its "lookback time." A galaxy at z ≈ 14, the kind of target in this lab, is seen as it was only a couple hundred million years after the Big Bang.

Reading a spectrum instead of a picture

A photo of a galaxy tells you its shape, but not its distance. To measure redshift precisely, astronomers instead spread a galaxy's light out into a spectrum — brightness as a function of wavelength — using JWST's NIRSpec instrument. Hot gas inside a galaxy, ionised by young stars, emits light very strongly at specific, well-known wavelengths: hydrogen's Hα and Hβ lines, doubly-ionised oxygen's [OIII] line, and several others. In the galaxy's own rest frame, these lines always sit at the same wavelengths — physics doesn't change across the universe. What changes is where those lines show up in our spectrum, because the whole spectrum has been redshifted together.

Finding the redshift is therefore a matter of pattern matching: find the value of z that shifts the known rest-frame positions of these emission lines onto the bright peaks actually seen in the data. Because several lines have known relative spacings, a good redshift lines up all of them simultaneously — a single matching line could be a coincidence, but four or five lines lining up together is essentially unambiguous.

How the lab works

1
Choose a real JWST/NIRSpec spectrum. Each button loads the measured spectrum of a real JADES galaxy — some nearby and bright, some among the most distant galaxies ever confirmed.
2
Drag the redshift slider. As you change z, the coloured dashed lines marking known emission-line wavelengths slide across the plot in real time — they're being redshifted exactly the way light really is.
3
Line them up. Your job is to find the redshift where the dashed lines fall precisely on top of the real bumps and peaks in the black spectrum — that's the galaxy's true redshift.
4
Check your score. "Show Score" converts the difference between your guess and the true redshift into a velocity offset in km/s (using Δv = c · Δz / (1+z)) — the same way astronomers quote how precisely a redshift has been measured. Within ±1000 km/s is an excellent match.

Why it matters: the race to the cosmic dawn

One of JWST's flagship goals — and a central aim of the JADES survey — is finding and confirming the most distant, earliest galaxies that exist, right at the "cosmic dawn" when the first stars and galaxies were forming. A galaxy's brightness and colour in an image alone can only give a rough, uncertain estimate of its redshift; only a spectrum, with real emission lines matched with confidence, can confirm it. Every record-breaking distant galaxy JWST has announced was confirmed exactly this way — matching known lines to a measured spectrum, just like you're doing in this lab.

Redshift (z)
The fractional stretch of light's wavelength caused by the universe's expansion during its journey to us; larger z means further away and further back in time.
Emission line
A wavelength at which hot gas emits light very strongly, at a fixed, known rest-frame wavelength.
Lookback time
How long ago the light we're observing was actually emitted.
Cosmic dawn
The era, a few hundred million years after the Big Bang, when the first stars and galaxies formed.
