What is photometry?

"Photometry" simply means measuring light. When the James Webb Space Telescope points at a distant galaxy, it doesn't take a snapshot the way a phone camera does — every pixel of the image records a precise brightness value, the number of photons that landed on the detector during the exposure. By carefully modelling how that brightness is arranged across the galaxy, astronomers can work out its size, its shape, and how tightly its stars are packed together — without ever needing to travel there.

Galaxies aren't all built the same way

If you plot the brightness of a galaxy against distance from its centre, you get a curve that falls off smoothly outward — but the exact shape of that fall-off is different for different kinds of galaxies. Flat, star-forming disk galaxies (like our own Milky Way) fade gently. Puffy, old elliptical galaxies, built up from mergers of smaller galaxies, are very concentrated in the centre and fade much more sharply. In 1968, the astronomer José Sérsic proposed a single mathematical formula flexible enough to describe both extremes — and everything in between:

The Sérsic profile

Brightness falls off with distance r from the galaxy's centre roughly as I(r) ∝ exp(-r1/n). The single number n, the Sérsic index, controls the shape of that fall-off:

  • n ≈ 1 — a smooth exponential disk, typical of spiral / star-forming galaxies.
  • n ≈ 4 — a steep, centrally concentrated profile, typical of elliptical galaxies and the bulges of massive galaxies.

Alongside n, astronomers also fit an effective radius (the size within which half the galaxy's light is contained), an ellipticity and position angle (how squashed the galaxy looks, and which way it's tilted on the sky), and a central position and brightness. Together, these numbers are a compact summary of a galaxy's structure — and comparing them across thousands of galaxies at different cosmic epochs is one of the main ways we learn how galaxies grow and change shape over billions of years of cosmic history.

Why it's harder than it sounds: the PSF

No telescope is perfect. Even a point of light — a single unresolved star — is smeared out slightly by the telescope's optics into a small blob called the point spread function, or PSF. For a very distant, compact galaxy, this blurring can be a significant fraction of its apparent size, so a fair model has to include it. That's why, in the lab, your Sérsic model isn't compared to the image directly — it's first blurred by JWST's PSF (the same way the real light was blurred by the telescope) before being compared to what NIRCam actually recorded.

How the lab works

1
Pick a real JWST galaxy. The buttons across the top load real NIRCam imaging of galaxies discovered by the JADES survey, at different distances and with different shapes.
2
Build your model. Seven sliders control your Sérsic model: peak brightness, size, Sérsic index, orientation angle, x/y position, and ellipticity. Every time you move a slider, the lab regenerates your model, blurs it by JWST's PSF, and redraws it.
3
Compare three panels. The Observed Image is the real data. Your Model is what your current sliders produce. The Difference panel shows what's left over once your model is subtracted from the data — red and blue patches mean your model is too faint or too bright in that spot.
4
Chase a low score. The score is a reduced chi-squared statistic — it measures, on average, how many "standard deviations" your model is away from the real data at each pixel. A score close to 1 means an excellent fit; a large score means there's still real structure in the difference panel that your model hasn't captured.

Why this matters

This is essentially the same fitting process used in real JADES research, just automated and applied to thousands of galaxies at once instead of by hand with sliders. By measuring how the sizes and shapes of galaxies change with cosmic time, astronomers can test theories of how galaxies assemble: whether they grow smoothly from the inside out, or whether mergers between galaxies play the dominant role in building the massive ellipticals we see in the local universe today.

Sérsic index (n)
A single number describing how centrally concentrated a galaxy's light is.
Effective radius
The radius of the circle containing half of a galaxy's total light.
Point Spread Function (PSF)
The blurred image a telescope produces of an idealised point of light, set by the telescope's optics.
Reduced chi-squared
A statistic comparing model to data, accounting for measurement noise; close to 1 indicates a good fit.
