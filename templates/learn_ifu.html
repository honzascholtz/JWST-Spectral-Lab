A spectrum for every pixel

A normal spectrograph gives you one spectrum — the combined light from an entire galaxy, or a single narrow slit across it. An Integral Field Spectrograph (IFS), like JWST's NIRSpec used in "IFU mode," does something much more powerful: it captures a full spectrum at every single pixel across a galaxy's image simultaneously. The result isn't a single image or a single spectrum, but a three-dimensional data cube — two spatial dimensions (x and y, across the sky) and one spectral dimension (wavelength), for every "spaxel" (spatial pixel) in the field. That means you can study how a galaxy's gas and light change not just from place to place, but precisely which wavelengths are doing what at each location.

Turning a shifted line into a velocity

Just as the redshift of an entire galaxy tells us how fast the universe carried it away from us (see the Redshift Lab), a small shift in an emission line's wavelength within a galaxy tells us how fast the gas at that specific point is moving toward or away from us, via the Doppler effect. Gas moving toward us is shifted slightly to shorter (bluer) wavelengths; gas moving away is shifted to longer (redder) wavelengths. By measuring the precise central wavelength of a bright emission line — here, doubly-ionised oxygen, [OIII] — at every spaxel across the galaxy, and comparing it to the line's expected position, astronomers can build a full 2D map of gas velocity across the galaxy.

Narrow and broad components

Emission lines aren't infinitely sharp — gas moving at different speeds along the line of sight smears a line out. Gas settled into a galaxy's rotating disk typically produces a narrow line, while gas caught up in a fast, turbulent outflow — often driven by supernovae or an actively feeding black hole — produces a much broader line, since it contains a wide spread of velocities. Fitting both a narrow and a broad component to the same line lets astronomers separate calm, rotating gas from violent outflowing gas in the same galaxy.

How the lab works

The data in this lab comes from a real GA-NIFS/JWST NIRSpec IFU observation of a distant galaxy, already reduced into maps and spectra.

1
Two summary maps. The left map shows the total [OIII] flux at every spaxel — in effect, a picture of where the ionised gas is brightest. The middle map shows the fitted gas velocity at every spaxel, colour-coded blue (approaching) to red (receding) — a direct picture of the galaxy's kinematics.
2
The wavelength slice map. The right-hand map shows the raw brightness of the cube at one specific wavelength (summed over a few adjacent channels), letting you see which spaxels are lighting up as you scan through the spectrum with the slider or the +/− buttons.
3
Click any spaxel. Clicking anywhere on the maps loads the full observed spectrum at that exact location, along with the best-fit model: the black "Observed" data, the red total "Model," and — where fitted — the green "Narrow" and purple "Broad" kinematic components separately.
4
Explore the pattern. Try clicking spaxels across the galaxy: do you see the velocity smoothly swing from blue to red across the disk, the signature of ordered rotation? Are there spots where the broad component becomes strong, hinting at an outflow of fast-moving gas?

Why it matters

Mapping motion, not just brightness, is how astronomers find rotating disks in the early universe, measure how much mass a galaxy has piled up by seeing how fast it spins, and catch galactic winds and outflows in the act — gas being blown out of a galaxy by intense star formation or a supermassive black hole, which can shut down future star formation entirely. Data cubes like this one, from surveys such as GA-NIFS, are one of the main tools astronomers use to understand not just what distant galaxies look like, but how they are physically assembling and evolving.

Integral Field Spectrograph (IFS/IFU)
An instrument that records a full spectrum at every spatial pixel across an image at once.
Spaxel
A "spatial pixel" — one (x, y) location in an IFU data cube, with its own full spectrum.
Doppler shift
The change in an emission line's wavelength caused by the motion of the gas producing it.
Outflow
Gas physically ejected from a galaxy at high speed, often traced by a broad emission-line component.
